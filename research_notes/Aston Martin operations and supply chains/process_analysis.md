## Q1. Current (2018–2026) process facts for Gaydon and St Athan, including Valhalla and Valkyrie build processes and new investments

### Takeaway
Gaydon is a **craft/indexed-station line process** for front-engined sports/GT cars. One of its two lines was **closed under Project Horizon (2020)**, so Gaydon has probably run **one main core-model line** since then (single secondary source). It is supplemented by **dedicated low-rate "Specials" build areas**. Valkyrie was built in a "specially commissioned Valkyrie production area" at Gaydon at **over 2,000 labour-hours per car** (about 10 times a core car's ~200 h). Valhalla (999 units) began customer deliveries in **Q4 2025** (152 delivered that quarter) and has run at roughly **2 cars per working day** in 2026 [calc]. St Athan remains the DBX-only plant (three hangars: BIW, paint, final assembly; 20-min takt; 85 h flow time). No 2023–2026 source gives current station counts, shift patterns or line speed for the DB12/Vantage/Vanquish line. That gap is still open.

### Inferences
- **Process type by product family (for the flowchart and the product–process matrix):**
  - Core GT/sports (Gaydon) = **low-volume, high-variety line flow**: an indexed, paced line in the "batch/line" zone.
  - DBX (St Athan) = a more automated, faster-takt (20 min) **paced line** that sits further toward "mass" on the matrix.
  - Valkyrie and Valhalla = **jobbing/project-like build cells** in dedicated areas, with very high labour content (Valkyrie >2,000 h) and low rates.
  - This is a **"plant-within-a-plant" focus strategy** (Skinner 1974) [inference].
- **Valhalla rate [calc]:** 225 Specials (H1 2026, mostly Valhalla) + 127 (Q3) ≈ **350 cars in ~190 working days ≈ 1.8 cars/day (~9/week)**. The 999-car run would then take about 2.5–3 years from Q4 2025. This assumes nearly all H1 Specials were Valhalla, which is not confirmed.
- **Labour-content contrast [calc]:** Valkyrie at ≥2,000 h is about **10×** a core Gaydon car (~200 h). Valhalla is probably between the two (no figure found). On a value-stream map, Specials should be drawn as a **separate value stream**, not a variant of the core line.
- With one core line since 2020 (if the Horizon closure stuck) and ~20 cars/day reported in 2024, Gaydon's line is likely running about 1.5–2 shifts at a 25–40 min takt. This is inference only. See the Q4 takt calculation.

## Q2. Order-to-delivery (order fulfilment) process: configurator, dealer order, order bank, slot allocation, lead times for core, Q Commission and Specials; delivery logistics

### Takeaway
AML operates a **build-to-order (BTO) system through independent franchised dealers** (c.156 retailers in 53 markets, 2025). Under Project Horizon (2020) it moved deliberately from build-to-stock to BTO. Customer-reported **order-to-delivery lead times for core cars (DB12, Vantage, DB12 S) cluster at about 5–8 months** (2024–2026, forum evidence only). **Q Collection adds no lead time.** **Q Commission adds "a few weeks" to "several months"** and must be agreed before production. **Specials** are pre-sold against deposits, years ahead of build. Outbound logistics to export markets runs by sea (e.g. a US east-coast ship arrival about 4 months after a 2024 order). No official lead-time figure is published.

### Inferences
- **Order-fulfilment flowchart (synthesised; each step evidenced, sequence inferred):**
  1. Customer configures (online configurator / dealer / Gaydon or Mayfair for Q Commission).
  2. Dealer places order into the AML order bank. Deposit taken; for Specials, allocation is made years ahead.
  3. **Decision: Q Commission?** Yes → design consultation → feasibility and sign-off → spec freeze (adds weeks to months). No → standard spec.
  4. Build slot allocated and **VIN assigned** (forum evidence implies VIN assignment marks the frozen-order point).
  5. Parts call-off (MRP against bill of materials; long-lead AMG engines, seats, electrical/electronic (E/E) modules).
  6. Build: Gaydon or St Athan (body → paint → trim/final → test).
  7. Quality gate / rectification (see Q3: 65% right-first-time (RFT) in 2024).
  8. Dispatch: road to UK dealers, or sea freight for export (≈4–8 weeks to the US).
  9. Dealer pre-delivery inspection (PDI) → customer handover.
- **Lead-time decomposition [calc, illustrative]:** a ~5–6 month core-car order-to-delivery time against a factory flow time of about 1–3 weeks (see the Q4 Little's Law calculation) means **the factory accounts for perhaps 5–15% of customer lead time**. Most of the wait is **queue time in the order bank** (slot allocation), plus parts lead time and outbound shipping. In value-stream terms, the process is dominated by non-value-adding waiting, which is normal for a BTO luxury OEM managing scarcity. Lead time can be cut by sequencing and capacity decisions rather than faster assembly.
- Forum reports of "factory not at capacity" yet 5–8 month quotes (2025–26) suggest **order-bank batching and spec-freeze windows**, rather than capacity, set the lead time. This is unverified.
- The **customer order decoupling point (CODP)** is at specification/final assembly for core cars (assemble/make-to-order). It moves upstream to **engineer-to-order** for Q Commission and Specials.

### Gaps
- **No official AML order-to-delivery lead time, frozen-window length, order-bank cover in months (post-2024), or slot-allocation rules** were found. All lead-time figures are customer forum anecdotes (low reliability, small samples, US-heavy).
- No source on PDI content, dispatch yard, vehicle-logistics provider, or shipping lanes and ports for AML specifically.
- No data on the split between build-to-order and build-to-stock (dealer stock orders) for core models.
- No source on the configurator-to-ERP order interface.

## Q3. Empirical evidence of process performance: quality, rework, E/E and software delays, cars awaiting parts, output shortfalls, productivity, lean and Industry 4.0 initiatives

### Takeaway
The strongest new quantified evidence is AML's own **right-first-time (RFT) metric**. It was **~65% in mid/H2-2024** (CEO Adrian Hallmark: 55–65%, "nowhere near industry norms") and rose to **95% by H2 2025**, at a cost of about **£65m** extra warranty, dealer-support and quality spend. The process repeatedly **starved final completion for lack of parts**:
- **350+ DBX707s** awaiting final parts at end-June 2022 (≈£80m cash tied up)
- DB12 E/E-platform readiness delays in 2023
- c.1,000 fewer cars in 2024 after supplier failures

Productivity is low and falling (≈1.9 cars and ≈£0.45m revenue per employee in 2025 [calc]). Digital initiatives found are an **Infor cloud ERP (~2020)** with machine-learning quality alerting, and a Siemens WinCC SCADA. The brief's "Microsoft Dynamics" was **not confirmed**.

### Cited Findings

**Quality: right-first-time (RFT)**
- "Right-first-time metric increased from **65% in the middle of 2024 to 95%** by the second half of 2025". The same figures appear in the Annual Report. The H1 2025 presentation gives the baseline as **c.65% during H2'24** (slight baseline-date discrepancy) [extract] — [AML FY2025 results, 25 Feb 2026](https://www.astonmartin.com/-/media/corporate/documents/fy-results-2025/aston-martin-lagonda-fy-2025-results.pdf?rev=cfee91d19eda4499b80f930b2012072c); [AML Annual Report 2025](https://www.astonmartin.com/-/media/corporate/corporatearchive/documents/annual-reports/aston-martin-lagonda-annual-report-2025.pdf?rev=481f11aaed454601b437f2d2730eb377); [AML H1 2025 results presentation, 30 Jul 2025](https://www.astonmartin.com/-/media/corporate/documents/h1-results-2025/aston-martin-lagonda-h1-2025-results-presentation.pdf?rev=262b14e7c90f4d7c8f9a300a39a8e2e3)

### Inferences
- **Hidden factory [calc]:** at 65% RFT on 2024's 6,030 cars, about **2,100 cars (35%)** needed off-line rectification. At 95% on 2025's 5,448 cars, about **270**. The 2024 rectification loop was therefore effectively a **second, unplanned process stage** as large as a third of the line's output. In Theory of Constraints (TOC) terms, rectification capacity may have been the system constraint in 2024. This assumes RFT is measured per car at end of line, and AML's definition was not captured.
- **350 DBX707s stranded [calc]:** at H1 2022 group throughput (2,676 cars over ~125 working days ≈ 21 cars/day), 350 cars ≈ **16 working days of group output** sitting as near-finished WIP. Against ≈£80m cash, that is ≈£230k per car, which suggests the cash figure includes the value of undelivered revenue/receivables, not just cost. A classic **"kitted-but-incomplete" failure** of sequenced supply: one missing dependent-demand part (reportedly chips) blocks completion and inflates WIP, which Little's Law then turns directly into longer lead time.
- **Productivity [calc; assumptions flagged]:**
  - **Cars per employee:** 2025 = 5,448 / ~2,807 ≈ **1.9** (≈1.8 on a ~3,000 base).
  - **Revenue per employee:** 2025 ≈ £1,257.7m / 2,807 ≈ **£448k** (≈£419k on 3,000).
  - **Crude total manufacturing labour per car:** if ~2,500 manufacturing staff (2024) each work ~1,650 productive hours/yr, that is ≈4.1m h / 6,030 cars ≈ **~680 h per car**, about 3.4× the ~200 h direct-build figure. The gap would reflect indirect and support labour, rectification (65% RFT), Specials (Valkyrie ~2,000 h) and under-utilised capacity. This is a sanity check, not a measured statistic; the "manufacturing staff" definition is unknown.
- **Pattern for the process analysis:** performance failures cluster at **interfaces**. These are supplier → line (parts, E/E modules) and line → customer (RFT, software). They do not sit in the craft core (paint, trim). This argues for a flowchart with explicit **quality gates and a rectification loop**, and for treating E/E software flashing/validation as a distinct process step on new-architecture cars (DB12 onwards).

## Q4. Process-design theory and empirical literature to apply (with AML data plugged in) and peer process benchmarks

### Takeaway
Classic process-design tools map cleanly onto AML's data:
- **Product–process matrix** (Hayes & Wheelwright, 1979): core cars sit on the diagonal as a low-volume "line/batch"; Specials sit off-line as "job shop/project".
- **Takt**: Gaydon ~39 min (2018), St Athan 20 min / 40 min in body. The body cure is the designed-in constraint, which **TOC** "subordinates" via extra body-shop shifts.
- **Little's Law**: 85 h St Athan flow at a 20-min takt implies ~255 units in process. AML's £33.2m WIP (2023) implies about 1–3 weeks of factory flow time against a 5–8 month customer lead time.

Peer benchmarks:
- Ferrari: up to ~1 h per V12 station, ~4 days end-to-end (2013), 13,752 cars in 2024, new e-building capacity ~20,000/yr.
- McLaren: ~20 cars/day designed, 16–25 working days to build.
- Bentley: Mulsanne 400 h (136 h trim).
- Rolls-Royce: Phantom final assembly ~11 stations × 2 h, ~45 days minimum.

AML's ~200 h per car and ~20 cars/day sit between McLaren-style low-rate and Ferrari-style mid-rate craft lines.

### Inferences (applied calculations, all [calc])

- **Little's Law, St Athan:**
  - Throughput at a 20-min takt = 3 cars per line-hour. Flow time W = 85 line-hours. So L = λW ≈ **255 bodies/cars in process** (BIW through end of line), if the 85 h is measured in operating hours.
  - If the 85 h is calendar hours, operating at ~16 h/day, L is lower. At planned 4,000 cars/yr (~17 cars/day over 240 days) and ~85 h ≈ 3.5–5.3 working days, L ≈ **60–90 cars**.
  - Either way, **cure and paint dwell dominate** WIP, not touch time.
- **Little's Law, group WIP:**
  - 2023 WIP = £33.2m. Cost of sales 2023 ≈ revenue £1,632.8m × (1 − 39.1% GM) ≈ £994m, or ≈£150k per car over 6,620 cars.
  - Valuing average WIP at 50–100% of full car cost gives **≈220–440 cars in WIP**.
  - At 6,620 cars / ~240 days ≈ 27.6 cars/day, factory **flow time W = L/λ ≈ 8–16 working days**.
  - Compare the ~5–8 month order-to-delivery time: **factory flow is ~5–15% of customer lead time**. This supports Holweg & Pil's (2004) BTO finding at AML.
- **Takt time (illustrative formula for the essay):**
  - Takt = available production time ÷ customer demand rate.
  - Example: Gaydon core demand ~3,000 cars/yr (unsourced assumption; the DBX/Gaydon split was not found) over 230 days × 1 shift of 450 min → takt ≈ **34.5 min**; with 2 shifts ≈ 69 min.
  - The reported 39-min takt (2018) and ~20 cars/day (2024) are consistent with ~1.5–2 shifts on one line. The required number of stations ≈ total work content ÷ (takt × operators per station). For 200 h ÷ (39 min × ~3 operators) ≈ **~100 operator-station positions**. This is a theoretical line-balancing figure, not a verified station count.
- **TOC applied:**
  1. Identify: the **body adhesive cure** (40 vs 20 min).
  2. Exploit: separate ovens, no idle cure time.
  3. Subordinate: the body shop runs **two shift patterns** to feed a single-shift downstream rate.
  4. Elevate: more ovens or faster-cure adhesives.
  - The 2022–24 evidence shows the **operational constraint migrated outside the plant** (parts, E/E software) and, in 2024, to **rectification** (65% RFT). This is the TOC lesson that constraints move once one is fixed.
- **Product–process matrix positioning:** core GT/sports cars = low volume (~3–4k/yr), high variety. **Gaydon's indexed craft line sits on the diagonal between "batch" and "line"**, like Ferrari's Maranello lines (~1 h/station) and Rolls-Royce's 2 h stations. St Athan's 20-min, robot-painted line sits further toward "line/mass" for the higher-volume DBX. Valkyrie/Valhalla build areas sit in the **job-shop/project** region. Running all three on one site risks the mismatch Hayes & Wheelwright warn against, unless physically separated (plant-within-a-plant), which AML has done with a dedicated Specials area.
- **Benchmark reading:** AML's ~200 labour-h per core car is ≈8–12× the IMVP volume-plant HPV (17–25). That is consistent with craft paint and trim, and of the same order as Rolls-Royce (~260 h, uncertain) and below Bentley Mulsanne (400 h). The weak points relative to peers are **dependability and RFT**, not hours. Ferrari's 2024 output (13,752) is ≈2.5× AML's 2025 (5,448) on a comparable craft-line model, and Ferrari reports no comparable stranded-WIP or RFT crises in the sources found.
