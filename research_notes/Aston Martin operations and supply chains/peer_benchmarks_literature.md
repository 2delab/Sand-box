# Peer Benchmarks and Academic Empirical Literature: Aston Martin Lagonda (AML) vs Luxury/Low-Volume Peers

Compiled 6 November 2026. Notes on method and caveats:
- The network proxy blocked direct fetches from astonmartin.com, cdn.ferrari.com, sec.gov and api.crossref.org. So almost every company figure below comes from search-engine extraction of the primary documents (AML RNS/annual reports, Ferrari press releases/20-F, VW Group/Audi releases, BMW PressClub, Porsche newsroom). Figures taken only from a search extract are marked **[extract]**. Figures I derived arithmetically are marked **[derived]**. Bibliographic details I could not confirm in this session (they come from prior knowledge) are marked **[recall – verify DOI before citing]**.
- Currencies differ: AML and McLaren report in GBP, Ferrari, VW brands and Porsche in EUR. Rolls-Royce publishes no stand-alone P&L because BMW does not segment it. Margins are **not like-for-like**:
  - AML reports *adjusted EBITDA* and *adjusted EBIT*.
  - Ferrari reports EBITDA and EBIT, both group-wide and including Formula 1 and sponsorship.
  - VW reports brand *operating result / return on sales (RoS)*, which is close to EBIT.
  - Porsche AG reports group operating RoS, which includes financial services.
- "Revenue per car" is total revenue divided by units. It includes non-vehicle revenue (Ferrari sponsorship/brand/F1; AML brand and motorsport; Porsche financial services), so it overstates vehicle ASP. That overstatement is largest for Ferrari.
- Unit definitions differ: AML = wholesales (to dealers); Ferrari = shipments; Lamborghini, Bentley, Rolls-Royce and Porsche = retail deliveries to customers; McLaren = retail/wholesale (unclear).

## Q1. Quantitative peer benchmark table, 2022–2025

### Takeaway
On every scale and profitability metric, AML is the weakest of the profitable peers:
- **Volume:** 5,448 wholesales in FY2025, against Ferrari 13,640, Lamborghini 10,747 and Bentley 10,131. Only Rolls-Royce (~5,664) and McLaren (~2,000) are similar in size.
- **Revenue per unit:** about £231k, roughly €270k at the FY25 average rate (~€1.17/£) [derived; FX is an assumption]. Ferrari is about €524k and Lamborghini about €297k.
- **Margins:** gross margin 29.4%, adjusted EBITDA margin 8.6%, adjusted EBIT margin −15.0% in FY2025. Ferrari's EBIT margin is 29.5% and Lamborghini's RoS is 24.0%.
- **Trend:** AML margins fell in every year from 2023 to 2025. Ferrari widened its margin while holding volume deliberately flat.
- **Closest analogue:** Bentley is the nearest "volume-stressed" peer. Its RoS fell from 20.1% (2023) to 14.1% (2024) and 8.3% (2025) as deliveries dropped from 15,174 (2022) to 10,131 (2025).
- **The lesson:** low volume alone does not cause poor margins. Pricing power, product cadence and capital discipline drive the gap.

### Inferences
- AML has the lowest gross margin and adjusted EBITDA margin of the profitable peers in every year from 2023 to 2025. On a loose EBITDA versus RoS/EBIT comparison, its FY2025 adjusted EBITDA margin of 8.6% is about equal to Bentley's 2025 RoS of 8.3%, which is an EBIT-type measure. On a like-for-like EBIT basis, AML (−15%) is far below Bentley (+8.3%).
- Ferrari's revenue per unit is about twice AML's in EUR terms, despite only ~2.5× the volume. Its EBITDA margin has stayed around 38% throughout. The evidence points to pricing power, personalisation and order-book scarcity rather than scale economies alone.
- Bentley's 2023–2025 trajectory is the clearest peer analogue to AML. Both saw volume fall (Bentley −33% 2022→2025), both were exposed to China and the US, and both suffered tariff and FX headwinds while profit compressed. Lamborghini, with a fresher hybrid range (Revuelto, Urus SE, Temerario), kept a RoS of 24–27% at similar volume. This suggests product cadence and powertrain-transition timing matter as much as scale.
- Porsche's 2025 RoS collapse to 1.1% shows that even the most resilient volume-luxury peer was hit by the same 2025 shocks AML faced: China, US tariffs and an expensive BEV pivot.

## Q2. How peers organise operations and supply chains differently

### Takeaway
The contrast is between two philosophies:
- **Scarcity, build-to-order and steady volume.** Ferrari holds deliberate under-supply, an order book running ~2 years, flat volume during changeovers, in-house powertrains, and a new e-building that keeps ICE, hybrid and BEV on one flexible site. Rolls-Royce holds Goodwood daily output flat while redesigning the line for Bespoke.
- **Volume-led recovery.** Under the Second Century Plan, AML expanded to two plants and leaned on wholesales into dealers. That is closer to a push model, and AML later had to "balance production and demand".
- **Group-backed peers.** Bentley and Lamborghini use VW Group platforms and purchasing. Bentley also invests in a carbon-neutral Crewe site and BEV line.
- **McLaren** shows the downside: a sub-scale independent with low volume and a weak balance sheet ended up under a new owner (CYVN/Forseven).

### Inferences
- Ferrari's model works as a deliberate demand-exceeds-supply buffer. The order book absorbs demand shocks, so production can be level-scheduled and volume held flat through changeovers, which protects residual values and price. AML's wholesale (dealer-push) model and its "balance production and demand" language point to the opposite exposure: dealer inventory builds up and volume has to be cut when demand softens.
- Rolls-Royce and Ferrari invest to raise value per car (Bespoke, personalisation) rather than volume. That is a direct counter-example to the volume expansion in AML's Second Century Plan, which added St Athan for DBX.
- VW-owned Bentley and Lamborghini benefit from group platforms and purchasing scale that AML lacks. AML depends on a technical partnership with Mercedes-AMG for engines and electronics. Bentley's margin erosion shows that group backing alone does not protect against a product-cycle gap.

### Gaps
- No primary evidence was retrieved on Porsche's specific supply-chain resilience measures (semiconductor stock, dual sourcing, the 2024 aluminium supplier fire disruption). This needs the Porsche annual report.
- The Ferrari dealer network size and its allocation mechanism (how the waiting list is managed by dealers) were not confirmed in-session.
- Peer Scope 1+2 per-car intensity is unavailable, so a quantitative sustainability benchmark is limited to AML's own figure.

## Q3. Academic empirical literature: luxury and low-volume automotive operations and supply chains

### Takeaway
The empirical literature supports four broad claims:
- **Luxury.** Supply chains in luxury firms follow distinct strategies that depend on product and brand: craft versus fashion versus mass-luxury (Brun et al. 2008; Caniato et al. 2011).
- **Build-to-order.** BTO and short order-to-delivery are feasible and profitable in auto. BTO cars earn higher unit margins (Lawson, Pil & Holweg 2018), but adoption faces structural barriers (Holweg & Pil 2004; Roehrich et al. 2011).
- **Lean and inventory.** Lean production yields large productivity gains (Krafcik 1988; Womack et al. 1990). Leaner inventory is associated with better financial performance (Chen, Frank & Wu 2005; Capkun et al. 2009).
- **Resilience and ESG.** The 2020–23 semiconductor shortage was a systemic disruption that propagated through the multi-tier auto supply chain (Ramani et al. 2022; Frieske & Stieler 2022). Automotive ESG–performance evidence is mixed but mostly positive (Dincergok & Pirgaip 2025; Glova & Panko 2025; Bancu et al. 2025).

### Cited Findings

**Luxury supply chain management**
- Brun, A., Caniato, F., Caridi, M., Castelli, C., Miragliotta, G., Ronchi, S., Sianesi, A. and Spina, G. (2008) 'Logistics and supply chain management in luxury fashion retail: empirical investigation of Italian firms', *International Journal of Production Economics*, 114(2), pp. 554–570. doi:10.1016/j.ijpe.2008.02.003.
  - Finding: a multiple case study of Italian luxury fashion firms. It identifies the critical success factors of luxury (quality, design, brand, exclusivity, service) and maps them onto logistics and SCM practices, showing that SC choices must be consistent with the brand's value proposition.
  - Source: [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925527308000455); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/proeco/v114y2008i2p554-570.html)
- Caniato, F., Caridi, M., Castelli, C. and Golini, R. (2011) 'Supply chain management in the luxury industry: a first classification of companies and their strategies', *International Journal of Production Economics*, 133(2), pp. 622–633. doi:10.1016/j.ijpe.2011.05.001 [DOI suffix recall – verify; ScienceDirect PII S0925527311002118 confirmed].
  - Finding: case studies of 15 Italian luxury companies across sectors. Five variables yield four clusters with distinct SC strategies and manufacturing, sourcing and distribution practices. The clusters separate craft-based "exclusive" luxury from more industrialised "accessible" luxury.
  - Source: [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:proeco:v:133:y:2011:i:2:p:622-633); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Supply-chain-management-in-the-luxury-industry:-A-Caniato-Caridi/ab1cc0917889ad655986c6d2a3f7674b56887cb9)

**Build-to-order and order-to-delivery in automotive**
- Holweg, M. and Pil, F.K. (2004) *The Second Century: Reconnecting Customer and Value Chain through Build-to-Order*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. ISBN 9780262083287 [recall – verify].
  - Finding: draws on the UK 3DayCar research programme. Most European and US cars are built to forecast and sold from stock, at large cost in discounts and inventory. Order-to-delivery is driven more by order-processing and scheduling information flows than by physical production. The authors argue for a BTO "second century" model.
  - Source: [University of Bath case study on 3DayCar/BTO research](https://bath.ac.uk/case-studies/producing-build-to-order-cars-in-five-days)
- Lawson, B., Pil, F.K. and Holweg, M. (2018) 'Multi-modal order fulfillment: concept and application', *Production and Operations Management*, 27(2), pp. 269–284. doi:10.1111/poms.12801.
  - Finding: on 48,534 individual vehicle sales from one global automaker, BTO orders earn higher unit profit margins than stock sales. This supports mixing BTO with stock fulfilment.
  - Source: [Zendy/DOI record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/poms.12801); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:popmgt:v:27:y:2018:i:2:p:269-284) [extract]
- Roehrich, J.K., Parry, G. and Graves, A. (2011) 'Implementing build-to-order strategies: enablers and barriers in the European automotive industry', *International Journal of Automotive Technology and Management*, 11(3), pp. 221–235. doi:10.1504/IJATM.2011.040869.
  - Finding: from the EU ILIPT ("5-day car") project, the paper identifies organisational, supplier and IT barriers to BTO and proposes a transition framework.
  - Source: [Inderscience](https://www.inderscience.com/filter.php?aid=40869); [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/implementing-build-to-order-strategies-enablers-and-barriers-in-t/)

**Lean production in automotive**
- Krafcik, J.F. (1988) 'Triumph of the lean production system', *Sloan Management Review*, 30(1), pp. 41–52 [recall – verify].
  - Finding: IMVP benchmarking of assembly plants worldwide. "Lean" (Toyota-style) plants achieved markedly higher productivity and quality than "buffered" mass-production plants, independent of automation level or location. The paper coined "lean production".
  - Source: [MIT Sloan Management Review](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/triumph-of-the-lean-production-system/) [URL recall – verify]
- Womack, J.P., Jones, D.T. and Roos, D. (1990) *The Machine That Changed the World*. New York: Rawson Associates [recall – verify].
  - Finding: the IMVP global assembly-plant survey. Japanese lean plants needed far fewer assembly hours per vehicle, with fewer defects, than US and especially European volume plants. The book singles out European luxury makers' heavy end-of-line rework as a lean anti-pattern, which is directly relevant to craft-heavy luxury producers like AML.
  - Source: [Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/store/book/the-machine-that-changed-the-world/) [URL recall – verify]
- Holweg, M. (2007) 'The genealogy of lean production', *Journal of Operations Management*, 25(2), pp. 420–437. doi:10.1016/j.jom.2006.04.001 [recall – verify].
  - Finding: a historical account of how lean emerged from Toyota and IMVP. It is useful for framing lean adoption in low-volume settings.
  - Source: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jom.2006.04.001)

**Leagile supply chains**
- Naylor, J.B., Naim, M.M. and Berry, D. (1999) 'Leagility: integrating the lean and agile manufacturing paradigms in the total supply chain', *International Journal of Production Economics*, 62(1–2), pp. 107–118. doi:10.1016/S0925-5273(98)00223-0 [recall – verify].
  - Finding: lean upstream and agile downstream of a decoupling point. This is the conceptual basis for luxury car makers that lean-produce bodies-in-white and powertrains but configure to order.
  - Source: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5273(98)00223-0)
- Christopher, M. and Towill, D. (2001) 'An integrated model for the design of agile supply chains', *International Journal of Physical Distribution & Logistics Management*, 31(4), pp. 235–246. doi:10.1108/09600030110394914 [recall – verify].
  - Finding: a hybrid lean/agile strategy with a decoupling point and postponement. Lean suits predictable, high-volume components and agile suits volatile, customised demand.
  - Source: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/09600030110394914)
- I found no automotive-specific empirical study labelled "leagile" in this session. — searched; see Gaps.

**Supply chain resilience, COVID-19 and the semiconductor shortage**
- Ramani, V., Ghosh, D. and Sodhi, M.S. (2022) 'Understanding systemic disruption from the Covid-19-induced semiconductor shortage for the auto industry', *Omega*, 113, 102720. doi:10.1016/j.omega.2022.102720 [authors and title verified; journal volume and DOI recall – verify].
  - Finding: models how the chip disruption started, propagated across tiers and persisted. Demand-forecast cuts by OEMs in 2020, long fab lead times and low auto bargaining power with foundries turned a demand shock into a multi-year systemic supply shortage.
  - Source: [Record (PubMed-indexed)](https://ebusca.uv.mx/Record/pubmed-9363154)
- Frieske, B. and Stieler, S. (2022) 'The "Semiconductor Crisis" as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic and impacts on the automotive industry and its supply chains', *World Electric Vehicle Journal*, 13(10), 189. doi:10.3390/wevj13100189.
  - Finding: analyses the causes of the shortage and its impacts on OEMs and suppliers. Through German industry evidence it shows coping strategies: direct OEM–chipmaker relationships, stockpiling, redesign and component standardisation.
  - Source: [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2032-6653/13/10/189)

**Inventory and financial performance**
- Chen, H., Frank, M.Z. and Wu, O.Q. (2005) 'What actually happened to the inventories of American companies between 1981 and 2000?', *Management Science*, 51(7), pp. 1015–1031. doi:10.1287/mnsc.1050.0368 [recall – verify; the in-session search could not surface this record].
  - Finding: US manufacturing inventories fell substantially from 1981 to 2000, mainly in raw materials and work-in-process. Firms with abnormally high inventories had poor long-run stock returns; firms with slightly lower than average inventories performed best.
  - Source: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0368)
- Chen, H., Frank, M.Z. and Wu, O.Q. (2007) 'U.S. retail and wholesale inventory performance from 1981 to 2004', *Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*, 9(4), pp. 430–456. Companion study, confirmed in-session.
  - Source: [IDEAS/RePEc citation context](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/journl/hal-00493163.html) [extract]
- Capkun, V., Hameri, A.-P. and Weiss, L.A. (2009) 'On the relationship between inventory and financial performance in manufacturing companies', *International Journal of Operations & Production Management*, 29(8), pp. 789–806. doi:10.1108/01443570910977698.
  - Finding: on US manufacturing firms from 1980 to 2005, there is a significant positive correlation between inventory performance (lower total, raw-material, WIP and finished-goods inventory) and financial performance (gross and operating profit). The effect varies by inventory type and industry.
  - Source: [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/journl/hal-00493163.html); [EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/on-the-relationship-between-inventory-and-financial-performance-in-manufacturing-companies-capkun-vedran/10014790031)

**ESG and performance in automotive**
- Dincergok, B. and Pirgaip, B. (2025) 'Financial constraints and the ESG–firm performance nexus in the automotive industry: evidence from a global panel study', *Sustainability*, 17(15), 6985. doi:10.3390/su17156985.
  - Finding: a global automotive panel, 2008–2023. Higher ESG scores improve financial performance, and the effect is moderated by financial constraints. This is relevant to the highly leveraged AML.
  - Source: [Atılım University repository](https://ada.atilim.edu.tr/entities/publication/31ab1652-64ce-4603-bc7c-cbdbae4998fc) [extract]
- Glova, J. and Panko, M. (2025) [authors per extract], *International Journal of Financial Studies*, 13(2), 82. doi:10.3390/ijfs13020082 [DOI inferred from RePEc handle – verify].
  - Finding: on 60 STOXX Europe 600 automotive firms from 2011 to 2022, the social pillar raises ROA, environmental performance slightly lowers it, and governance has no significant effect.
  - Source: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:gam:jijfss:v:13:y:2025:i:2:p:82-:d:1651952) [extract]
- Bancu, Cunea and Dragomir (2025), *Journal of Accounting and Management Information Systems*. doi:10.24818/jamis.2025.01002.
  - Finding: on 75 automakers and 443 semiconductor firms from 2020 to 2022, the global ESG score is positively associated with automakers' financial performance.
  - Source: [JAMIS](https://jamis.ase.ro/?p=394) [extract]
- A study of 22 Tokyo-listed auto firms (2017–2021) found no ESG impact on Tobin's Q. This is counter-evidence; authors and title not captured. — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/5297331) [extract]

### Inferences
- **Luxury classification:** Caniato et al.'s clusters suggest AML sits between "exclusive craft" (Specials such as Valkyrie and Valhalla) and "accessible/industrialised luxury" (DBX, Vantage). That hybrid position argues for a segmented supply chain strategy, not a single model.
- **BTO margins:** Lawson, Pil and Holweg's BTO-margin finding, combined with Ferrari's order-book model, gives empirical backing for shifting AML from dealer wholesale push towards customer-order pull. Holweg and Pil and Roehrich et al. warn that the binding constraints are information flows and supplier flexibility, not assembly capacity.
- **Inventory:** the inventory–performance studies give a direct empirical basis for treating AML's dealer and finished-goods stock and its "balancing production and demand" as a profitability lever, not just a working-capital issue.
- **Resilience:** the semiconductor-crisis papers support the view that a low-volume buyer with little bargaining power suffers disproportionately in allocation crises. This is a structural resilience risk for AML relative to VW- or BMW-backed peers.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed empirical study specific to *low-volume* or luxury *automotive* manufacturing operations (e.g., Ferrari, Bentley, AML production systems) was found in this session. The luxury SCM evidence base is fashion and Italian-luxury dominated.
- No automotive-specific "leagile" empirical study was found. Only the conceptual papers above, from recall, are available.
- Full verification of the recall-flagged DOIs was impossible because Crossref was blocked. They should be checked before final citation.
