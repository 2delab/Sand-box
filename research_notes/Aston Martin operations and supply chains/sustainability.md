## Q1. Year-by-year operational performance data, 2020–2025 (emissions, energy, waste, water, health and safety, people)

### Takeaway
- **Operational emissions are flat and small.** Scope 1+2 (market-based) has held at about 7.5–9.2 kt a year (2022–2025), under 1% of the footprint. Near-zero market-based Scope 2 relies on renewable-electricity certificates (REGOs) and carbon credits.
- **Scope 3 dominates.** It is about 1.08–1.19 Mt a year, roughly half from use of sold products. The rest is mostly purchased goods and capital goods.
- **Intensity is worsening.** Scope 1 emissions per manufactured unit rose to 1.53 t in 2025, from 1.33 t in 2024 and 1.38 t in 2022, as output fell.
- **Some measures improved:** lost-time accidents (13 to 7), waste to landfill (near zero), and total waste (−14% in 2025).
- **People measures stalled or fell:**
  - women in leadership about 17% against a 25% target;
  - no new apprentices recruited in 2025;
  - the median gender pay gap is low (4.8%) but the mean bonus gap is large (38%).

### Inferences
- **Implied manufactured units.** Dividing Scope 1 by Scope 1 per unit gives about 6,400 units (2022), 6,600 (2023), 6,450 (2024) and 5,240 (2025). The rise in intensity in 2025 is a volume-denominator effect: fixed gas loads for heating and paint are spread over fewer cars. This is an operations-management point. Low and falling utilisation worsens environmental efficiency per car even when absolute emissions fall.
- **Scope 3 mix, derived arithmetic:**
  - 2025 Category 11 is about 1,076,848 − 536,264 = 540,584 t, about 50% of Scope 3.
  - If ex-Cat. 11 rose 32%, the 2022 ex-Cat. 11 figure was about 406,000 t and 2022 Category 11 about 683,000 t.
  - The total therefore fell only about 1% because fewer, and partly hybrid, cars were sold. The supply-chain portion grew strongly.
  - Purchased goods plus capital goods (about 44%) are about 474,000 t in 2025, about 59 times Scope 1+2.
  - Logistics (about 2%) is about 21,500 t.
- **Rating the operations levers:**
  - REGOs and credits make market-based Scope 2 almost zero. Location-based Scope 2 fell only 4% from 2022 to 2025, so physical grid-electricity use is barely changed.
  - Scope 1 gas, which needs physical change such as heat pumps or electrifying paint ovens, fell 9%. That is real progress but slow.
- **Apprentice pipeline.** Zero recruitment in 2025, and zero completions in 2024–25, is a weakening of the social pillar and of future skills capacity. It is consistent with cost cutting.
- **Gender pay gap.** A median hourly gap of about 5% is far below the UK average of about 13%; that average is not sourced here, so confirm it from ONS before using it. The rising mean gap (10.3% to 12.0%) and the 38% mean bonus gap show men are concentrated in the highest-paid and bonus-eligible senior roles. This matches the stalled women-in-leadership figure.

## Q2. Supply-chain sustainability: supplier code, ESG assessment, modern slavery, minerals, leather, aluminium, logistics

### Takeaway
AML's supply-chain sustainability rests on a policy stack:
- Code of Conduct (November 2023);
- Responsible Procurement Policy;
- Human Rights Policy, with OECD-aligned due diligence on conflict minerals (3TG);
- annual Modern Slavery Act statements.

It also has a few concrete projects:
- DHL Supply Chain moved UK inbound logistics to HVO, bio-LNG and electric vehicles in 2025;
- Bridge of Weir supplies traceable, LWG Gold leather (supplier claims);
- low-carbon aluminium is exploratory.

No public evidence was found that AML uses EcoVadis, Drive Sustainability/NQC SAQ, CDP Supply Chain or RMI membership, or of audit counts. Category 1 emissions rose about 32%. Supply-chain measures are therefore not yet effective at the scale of the problem.

### Inferences
- **The HVO project is real but tiny in Scope 3 terms:**
  - 148 t saved so far, and 663 t projected for 2026, against logistics emissions of about 21,500 t (2% of 1.08 Mt);

  - HVO's lifecycle benefit also depends on feedstock (waste oils versus virgin crops), which the case study does not state.
- **Policy without published outcomes.** The supply-chain architecture is conventional for a UK-listed firm (Code, RPP, Human Rights Policy, MSA statement). No KPIs were found: suppliers assessed, audits carried out, non-conformances or remediation. Legitimacy theory would predict this pattern of symbolic over substantive disclosure. It is a gap to note rather than proof of weak practice.
- **Battery minerals.** The policy is scoped to 3TG. Cobalt, lithium, nickel and graphite, which are relevant to PHEV and future Lucid-derived BEV batteries, are not explicitly covered in the extracts found. ### Inferences
**Effectiveness, by dimension**
- **Operations (Scope 1+2, waste, water, H&S): moderately effective.** Changes are measurable and in the right direction in absolute terms. They are partly certificate-based, and intensity is worsening with lower volumes.
- **Supply chain: low demonstrated effectiveness.**
  - Category 1 is up about 32%.
  - Projects are pilot-scale: HVO saves about 0.06% of Scope 3.
  - No supplier-assessment KPIs are published.
- **Product: ineffective so far.** The BEV is a decade late against the 2022 plan, and the only electrified product is a 999-unit PHEV.
- **Social: mixed to negative.** Safety is better and the median pay gap is low. But the diversity target was missed, apprentice intake was zero in 2025, and 20% of jobs were cut.

**Stakeholder trust**
- **Freeman (stakeholder theory) and Mitchell, Agle and Wood (salience).** Creditors and controlling shareholders have the most power and urgency, so they dominate. ESG-oriented investors and rating agencies send mixed signals:
  - disclosure raters (CDP, S&P) reward AML's transparency;
  - risk-management raters (MSCI) penalise its weak management of material issues.
  - The divergence reflects known ESG-rating disagreement. Berg, Kölbel and Rigobon (2022) is a standard reference for this, but it was not retrieved here.
- **Legitimacy theory (Suchman, 1995; Deegan, 2002).** "Racing. Green." in 2022 and the "carbon neutral manufacturing" claim in 2023 are classic legitimation moves. The quiet re-basing of targets, the SBTi exit and the dropped certification are a test of whether the legitimacy was substantive. AML's candid disclosures in AR 2025 partly protect its credibility. No regulatory or NGO challenge has yet arisen, perhaps because AML is low-profile and low-volume.
- **Elkington (triple bottom line).** The "profit" leg's failure has directly cut the "planet" leg (EV capex down £300m) and the "people" leg (20% job cuts, no apprentices). TBL commitments are path-dependent on financial resilience.
- **Ambiguous link to value.** The literature (Friede et al., Eccles et al.) implies weaker ESG may raise AML's cost of capital over time. AML itself flagged this risk in SR 2022. But AML's cost of capital is currently driven by leverage (12.8x) far more than ESG, so the ESG–value link is likely second-order for AML in 2025–26.

**Peer framing**
- AML's EV slippage is not unique. Bentley, Lamborghini and Rolls-Royce all retreated in 2024–26, which strengthens AML's defence that it is following the market.
- AML is weaker than Ferrari, which, though also ICE-heavy, has a hard absolute operational target and a profitable balance sheet to fund it.
- Peers inside VW or BMW groups can draw on group capital and fleet-wide compliance. AML cannot. This structural disadvantage bears on long-term viability.
